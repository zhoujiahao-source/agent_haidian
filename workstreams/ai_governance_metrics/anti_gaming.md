# Anti-Gaming Protocol

The governance system should assume that well-intended teams, vendors and metrics can drift toward optimisation of what is visible. Anti-gaming therefore operates as a first-class control, not a footnote.

## 1. Denominator governance

Maintain an append-only intake ledger of accepted, rejected, duplicate, deferred and closed issue candidates with reason codes. UAR denominator membership is governed by an eligibility rule and cohort snapshot, not by teams choosing which solved items to report.

## 2. Issue splitting / merging

- detect many near-identical issues from the same geography/outcome;
- link parent/child issue IDs;
- count an underlying issue once for headline UAR unless the original eligibility decision documented materially independent problems;
- freeze cohort definitions; post-hoc merging/splitting requires an auditable correction note.

## 3. Success-only publication

Dashboard and annual release include STOP, MODIFY, CONTINUE, SCALE, inconclusive, Responsible STOP, retired capabilities, incidents and unresolved issues. Public memory must not be a trophy cabinet.

## 4. Metric switching

Primary target, denominator, subgroup definitions and guardrails are versioned before outcome review. Changes create a new experiment version; old results stay visible.

## 5. Time-window cherry-picking

Publish declared horizons and cohort dates. Teams may add longer/shorter diagnostic views but cannot replace the registered UAR horizon after results are known.

## 6. Average masking

Require subgroup/worst-case views where meaningful. A positive mean does not override a serious safety/equity/privacy floor breach.

## 7. Hidden manual labour

Track material human interventions, overrides, escalations and staffing needed to maintain outcome quality. “AI automation” cannot claim efficiency while quietly shifting work into invisible operators.

## 8. Complaint suppression

Do not use raw complaint count as a standalone “good” metric. Track:

- feedback-channel visibility/accessibility;
- complaint rate with exposure denominator where possible;
- resolution time;
- upheld/valid complaint outcomes;
- repeat issue rate;
- opt-out/appeal friction.

A fall in complaints with worse channel visibility is not improvement.

## 9. Automation bias

Audit whether human reviewers systematically accept automated recommendations. High agreement is not inherently good. Sample cases where humans overrode, should have overridden, or lacked time/information to exercise authority.

## 10. Proxy drift

Periodically verify that operational KPIs still represent the original public problem. If a proxy becomes detached—e.g. throughput rises while actual accessibility falls—the proxy loses decision authority until recalibrated.

## 11. Transfer laundering

Capabilities moving across Learning Units receive new target-context baseline, risk review and revalidation. Source-area success cannot be copied into target-area metrics.

## 12. Vendor / lock-in gaming

Procurement or technical success cannot erase switching cost, portability limits or end-of-service risk. Track exit readiness and test export/decommission paths before dependency becomes critical.

## 13. Safety under-reporting

Near misses, failed safe stops, degraded fallback, late incident detection and rollback failures are part of audit evidence. Safety is not represented only by “number of injuries”.

## 14. Independent audit sampling

The audit layer should sample:

- denominator eligibility decisions;
- closed cases excluded from headline metrics;
- high-risk authorisation and override records;
- metric-version changes;
- subgroup/worst-case calculations;
- manual-intervention logs;
- complaints and appeal handling;
- transfer/revalidation decisions;
- vendor exit/rollback drills.

Any correction to a published metric must preserve the previous value/version and reason for correction.
